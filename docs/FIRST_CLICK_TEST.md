# First-click test: does the site get people to what they need, fast?

A short test to run with five passengers, before and after a design change
(the home sheet, a regrouped menu). It answers two questions: where do people
tap first, and how long until they have their answer? Written 6 November 2026
(docs/POST_EXPO_ROADMAP.md, "Design").

## Who and how

- Five people who use buses here, on their own phones, one at a time.
  Include at least one who does not use apps much and one who uses a
  wheelchair, a walking aid or a buggy.
- Open https://worthingbrightonbus.co.uk/ fresh: a private window, so no saved
  stops or settings. Say only "this is a site for buses on the coast".
- Read each task aloud once. Do not help, point or hint. If they are stuck for
  60 seconds, note it and move on.
- Time from the end of the task to the moment they say the answer. Note the
  first thing they tap, and every wrong turn.

## The four tasks

1. **Next bus home.** "You're at Shoreham High Street. When is the next bus
   towards Brighton?" Answer: the time on the board for High Street (towards
   Brighton).
2. **Cheapest ticket.** "What is the cheapest way to pay for a day of buses
   between Lancing and Brighton?" Answer: the ticket checker's cheapest option.
3. **Step-free.** "Can you get on a bus at Marine Parade, Worthing, without
   steps?" Answer: the stop's access row (step-free, or not recorded, and what
   that means).
4. **Is the 700 late?** "Is the 700 running late right now?" Answer: a 700 on
   the live map or a board, with its lateness.

## Reading the results

- **First tap right** (on the path to the answer) for at least four of five
  people is the aim for every task.
- **Median time:** under 30 seconds for tasks 1 and 4, under 60 for 2 and 3.
- A task most people fail is a design problem, not a passenger problem: note
  the words they used for what they were looking for, and use those words.
- Run the same four tasks after the change, with five different people, and
  compare the first taps and the median times. Five people per round find most
  of the problems; the numbers are a guide, not a statistic to publish.
